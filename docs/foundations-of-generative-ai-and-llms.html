Generative AI, Large Language Models, and Coding Agents

Chapter 1 Foundations of Generative AI and LLMs

This part of the book builds an understanding of large language models from the ground up, following the lifecycle of an LLM project:

Chapter Question it answers
This chapter What is an LLM, how does a transformer generate text, and how do prompts and generation settings shape the output?
Chapter 2 How are LLMs pre-trained, and what do model size, data, and compute buy?
Chapter 3 How do we adapt a model to our tasks, efficiently, and measure the result?
Chapter 4 How do we make a model helpful, honest, and harmless?
Chapter 5 How do we serve a model and connect it to data and tools responsibly?

These chapters began as study notes from the DeepLearning.AI / AWS course Generative AI with Large Language Models (Coursera). They have since been rewritten as a reference, checked against the primary papers cited in each chapter, and extended with later developments.

1.1 What generative AI is

Generative models learn the statistical structure of their training data and produce new samples from it: text, images, audio, video, or code. This book focuses on large language models (LLMs): neural networks trained on very large text corpora to predict the next token. Chatbots, code assistants, and writing tools are applications built on top of that one capability.

Key terms:

  • Foundation model. A model trained broadly enough to be adapted to many downstream tasks. Examples include BERT, GPT, T5/FLAN-T5, BLOOM, and Llama. Its capabilities must be measured on each task; they cannot be assumed from its size.
  • Parameters. The learned weights, currently up to hundreds of billions for large models. Parameters encode distributed statistical patterns, not a database of retrievable facts. More parameters add capacity, but data, training recipe, and adaptation matter as much.
  • Prompt, completion, inference. The text you send is the prompt. Producing text is inference. The output is the completion.
  • Context window. The maximum number of tokens the model can attend to at once: instructions, conversation, documents, tool results, and its own output. The limit varies by model; check current documentation.

You interact with an LLM in natural language, not in a formal programming language. You can use it as is, or adapt it (Chapter 3) without training a new model.

In short.

  • An LLM is a neural network trained on huge text corpora to predict the next token; chatbots and assistants are applications built on that.
  • Parameters store statistical patterns, not a fact database.
  • The context window caps everything the model can see at once.

1.2 What LLMs are used for

Many tasks reduce to next-token prediction under the right prompt:

  • Generation: drafting essays, emails, or marketing copy.
  • Summarization: condensing dialogues, documents, or reviews.
  • Translation: between natural languages, or from natural language to code or SQL.
  • Information extraction: pulling named entities (people, places, organizations), fields, or labels out of text.
  • Augmented tasks: answering from documents or calling APIs. These are not abilities of the model alone; the surrounding application supplies the data and tools (Chapter 5).

Larger models generally handle more tasks without examples. Smaller or specialized models can still be the better choice on cost, latency, privacy, or a narrow task. Choose by evaluating on your own use case.

In short.

  • Generation, summarization, translation, and extraction are all next-token prediction under the right prompt.
  • Answering from documents or calling APIs needs an application around the model.
  • Pick the model size by evaluation, not by default.

1.3 Before transformers: recurrent networks

Earlier language models used recurrent neural networks (RNNs). An RNN reads tokens one at a time and carries a hidden state forward, so in principle it summarizes everything seen so far. In practice it has three problems:

  • Long-range dependencies. Gradients shrink (or explode) as they flow back through many time steps, so plain RNNs struggle to learn from distant context (Bengio et al. 1994). LSTMs reduced but did not remove the problem (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997).
  • Sequential computation. Step \(t\) needs the result of step \(t-1\). Training cannot be parallelized across positions, which limits model and data scale.
  • A fixed-size bottleneck. The whole history must be squeezed into one state vector.

Language needs wide context. Words have several meanings: a bank can be a financial institution or a riverside. Sentences can be syntactically ambiguous: in “The teacher taught the students with the book”, whose book is it? Resolving such cases often requires the whole sentence or document.

Attention was the fix. It was first added to RNN translation models: the decoder learned to look back at all encoder states and weight the most relevant ones (Bahdanau et al. 2015). The transformer took the next step and dropped recurrence entirely.

In short.

  • RNNs read one token at a time: weak on long-range context, impossible to parallelize across positions, and limited by one state vector.
  • Attention lets the model look back at all earlier states directly.

1.4 The transformer

“Attention Is All You Need” (Vaswani et al. 2017) introduced the transformer. It is built from attention and position-wise feed-forward layers, with no recurrence. Two properties explain its success:

  • Parallelism. All positions in a sequence are processed at once. That makes training efficient on GPUs and allows much larger models and datasets.
  • Direct context. Every token can attend to every other token in one step, however far apart they are.

1.4.1 From text to vectors

  1. Tokenization. Text is split into tokens from a fixed vocabulary. Tokens are usually subword units learned by algorithms such as byte-pair encoding (Sennrich et al. 2016), so rare words split into pieces. Each token maps to an integer ID. A model must always be used with the tokenizer it was trained with. For example, a tokenizer might split “unbelievably” into un + believ + ably, while “the” is a single token (the exact split depends on the tokenizer). For English, a common rule of thumb is about 0.75 words per token, so 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words.
  2. Embedding. Each token ID is mapped to a learned vector of dimension \(d_{\text{model}}\) (512 in the original transformer). Tokens with related meanings end up close together in this space.
  3. Positional information. Attention by itself ignores order, so position must be added. The original paper added fixed sinusoidal positional encodings to the embeddings. Many modern LLMs instead use rotary position embeddings (RoPE), which encode relative position inside the attention computation (Su et al. 2024).

1.4.2 Self-attention

Each token’s vector \(x_i\) is projected into three vectors with learned matrices. The query \(q_i = x_i W^Q\) says what the token is looking for. The key \(k_j = x_j W^K\) says what each token offers. The value \(v_j = x_j W^V\) is what gets passed along if the token is attended to. Stacking these vectors into matrices \(Q, K, V\) gives scaled dot-product attention:

\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V \]

  • \(QK^\top\) scores how relevant every token is to every other token. The softmax turns each row of scores into weights that sum to one; plotted as a grid, these weights form an attention map. Each output is a weighted average of the value vectors.
  • Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)? Dot products grow with the vector dimension \(d_k\). Large scores push the softmax towards a one-hot output, where gradients become very small. Scaling keeps training stable.
  • Multi-head attention runs \(h\) attention operations in parallel, each with its own projections (the original used \(h = 8\) heads of size \(d_k = 64\)), and concatenates the results. Different heads learn different relations, for example syntactic agreement, coreference, or topic. Which relation a head learns is not chosen in advance.
  • Cost. The score matrix is \(n \times n\) for a sequence of \(n\) tokens, so attention compute and memory grow quadratically with context length. That is why long contexts are expensive. At 4,096 tokens the matrix has about 16.8 million entries per head and layer; at 131,072 tokens (32 times longer) it has about 17.2 billion, 1,024 times more. Optimized kernels such as FlashAttention avoid storing the full matrix, but the compute still grows quadratically.

Example: one attention step. In “The campaign missed its target because it launched late”, the token “it” should attend mostly to “campaign”. Suppose its scaled scores against “campaign”, “target”, and “late” are 2.0, 0.0, and 1.0. The softmax turns them into weights of about 0.67, 0.09, and 0.24. The output for “it” is then \(0.67\,v_{\text{campaign}} + 0.09\,v_{\text{target}} + 0.24\,v_{\text{late}}\): a vector that now carries mostly the meaning of “campaign”. (Real models attend over every token, and each head produces its own weights.)

1.4.3 The rest of a transformer block

Each layer (block) wraps attention with:

  • a position-wise feed-forward network: two linear layers with a nonlinearity, applied to each token separately (inner width 2,048 in the original);
  • residual connections and layer normalization around both sub-layers, which make deep stacks trainable.

The original model stacked six such layers in the encoder and six in the decoder. A final linear layer maps each output vector to one logit per vocabulary token, and a softmax turns the logits into a probability distribution over the next token.

1.4.4 Encoder, decoder, and the three model families

  • The encoder reads the whole input with bidirectional self-attention. It produces a contextual representation of each input token.
  • The decoder generates output one token at a time. Its self-attention is masked (causal): each position sees only earlier positions, so the model cannot peek at the token it is predicting. In an encoder–decoder model, the decoder also uses cross-attention, where its queries attend over the encoder’s outputs.

Using one or both halves gives three families: encoder-only (BERT), decoder-only (GPT, Llama), and encoder–decoder (T5, BART). They differ in pre-training objective and in which tasks they suit. Chapter 2 compares them in detail.

1.4.5 Generating text, end to end

Take translation with an encoder–decoder model, from “J’aime l’apprentissage automatique” to “I love machine learning”:

  1. Tokenize the French sentence with the model’s tokenizer.
  2. The encoder processes all input tokens in parallel into contextual representations.
  3. The decoder starts from a start-of-sequence token. Attending to its own previous outputs and (through cross-attention) to the encoder’s representations, it produces a distribution over the next token, and one token is chosen.
  4. The chosen token is appended and fed back in. Step 3 repeats until an end-of-sequence token is produced or a length limit is reached.
  5. Detokenize the output tokens into text.

A decoder-only model works the same way without the encoder. The prompt and the tokens generated so far form one sequence, and the model extends it one token at a time. This loop is autoregressive generation. How the next token is chosen from the distribution is set by the generation configuration (Section 1.6).

In short.

  • Tokens become embeddings plus position information.
  • Self-attention, \(\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V\), lets every token take a weighted average of every other token’s values; multi-head runs several in parallel; cost is quadratic in length.
  • Encoder (bidirectional), decoder (causal), encoder–decoder (cross-attention); generation is autoregressive, one token at a time.

1.5 Prompting and in-context learning

Prompt engineering is designing the input so the model produces the desired completion. Its most important effect, discovered at scale, is in-context learning (ICL): the model picks up a task from examples placed in the prompt, with no weight updates (Brown et al. 2020).

  • Zero-shot: an instruction and the input, no examples. For example, “Classify this review: … Sentiment:”.
  • One-shot: one solved example before the real input.
  • Few-shot: several solved examples, ideally covering the label space (for example, both a positive and a negative review).

A few-shot prompt for tagging customer comments:

Tag each comment as PRICE, DELIVERY, or PRODUCT.

Comment: "Arrived three days late and the box was crushed."
Tag: DELIVERY

Comment: "Great jacket, but $40 more than at the outlet."
Tag: PRICE

Comment: "The zipper broke after one week."
Tag:

The model continues with “PRODUCT”. The examples teach both the labels and the output format, without any training.

What to know:

  • ICL depends on scale. Large models often do well zero-shot. Small models may fail even with several examples.
  • Examples cost context. They use up the context window and add tokens to every call.
  • There is a ceiling. If a handful of examples does not get the performance you need, more examples rarely help. That is the signal to fine-tune (Chapter 3).

The Prompt Engineering part of this book (starting with Chapter 6) covers prompt structure, techniques, and evaluation in depth.

In short.

  • In-context learning: the model picks up a task from examples in the prompt, with no weight updates.
  • Zero-, one-, and few-shot differ only in the number of solved examples.
  • It depends on scale and costs context; if a few examples do not work, fine-tune.

1.6 Configuring generation

At each step the model outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary. Inference-time settings (as opposed to training parameters) decide how the next token is picked from it, and when to stop.

  • Max new tokens. A cap on the number of generated tokens. Generation can stop earlier at an end-of-sequence token.
  • Greedy decoding. Always pick the most probable token. It is deterministic and fine for short factual outputs, but prone to repetitive loops in longer text. (Beam search, which keeps the best few partial sequences, is a deterministic alternative common in translation.)
  • Random sampling. Draw the next token in proportion to its probability. This adds variety but can wander into low-probability, incoherent tokens. In Hugging Face Transformers, sampling is enabled with do_sample=True.
  • Top-k sampling. Sample only from the \(k\) most probable tokens, renormalized.
  • Top-p (nucleus) sampling. Sample from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches \(p\). The candidate set adapts to how confident the model is (Holtzman et al. 2020).
  • Temperature. Rescale the logits before the softmax: \(p_i \propto \exp(z_i / T)\). \(T < 1\) sharpens the distribution (more predictable). \(T > 1\) flattens it (more varied). \(T = 1\) leaves it unchanged.

Worked example. Suppose the next-token logits are apple \(= -1.0\), banana \(= 0.5\), cake \(= 2.0\), and donut \(= 1.2\).

Next token \(T = 0.5\) \(T = 1.0\) \(T = 2.0\)
apple 0.002 0.029 0.094
banana 0.040 0.130 0.200
cake 0.797 0.581 0.423
donut 0.161 0.261 0.283

At \(T = 1\):

  • Top-k with \(k = 3\) keeps cake, donut, and banana and renormalizes them to 0.598, 0.269, and 0.133.
  • Top-p with \(p = 0.8\) keeps cake and donut, the smallest set whose cumulative probability (0.842) reaches 0.8, and renormalizes them to 0.690 and 0.310.

Apple can never be sampled under either filter.

In practice, use low temperature or greedy decoding for extraction, classification, and code. Use moderate temperature with top-p for open-ended writing. Hosted APIs may expose only some of these settings, and reasoning models may fix them.

In short.

  • Generation settings choose the next token from the model’s distribution.
  • Greedy is deterministic; sampling adds variety; top-k and top-p cut off unlikely tokens; temperature sharpens (\(T<1\)) or flattens (\(T>1\)).
  • Low temperature for extraction and code, moderate with top-p for writing.

1.7 The generative AI project lifecycle

An LLM project moves through four stages, and later results often send you back to an earlier one:

Stage Key decisions Covered in
Scope Define the use case narrowly: which tasks, which inputs, what counts as success This section
Select Use an existing foundation model (the usual choice) or pre-train your own Chapter 2
Adapt and align Prompting first, then fine-tuning or PEFT, then alignment with human feedback; evaluate at every step Chapters 3 and 4
Integrate Optimize for inference; add retrieval and tools to overcome model limits; deploy, monitor, and manage risks Chapter 5

Scoping matters most. A model that must do many things needs to be larger and more general than one that only extracts named entities from invoices. A narrow scope makes model choice, evaluation, and risk assessment easier and cheaper.

The inner loop is adapt → evaluate → adapt. Start with prompt engineering and in-context learning. Measure against a held-out evaluation set. Fine-tune only if prompting falls short. Then return to prompting with the tuned model.

1.7.1 What each lever costs

The levers differ sharply in what they change, how long they take, and the skill they demand. That is why the usual order is cheapest first:

Pre-training Prompting Fine-tuning / PEFT Alignment (RLHF, DPO) Compression
What changes All weights, plus the architecture, tokenizer, and context length Nothing in the model; only the input All weights, or small adapter weights (Section 3.5) Weights or adapters, steered by preference data Model size or precision: pruning, quantization, distillation
Data needed A very large general or domain corpus None, or a few examples in the prompt Hundreds to many thousands of labeled task examples Preference comparisons from humans or AI, often with a reward model Little: a calibration set, or teacher outputs for distillation
Training time Weeks to months on large clusters None Minutes to days Similar to fine-tuning or longer None to hours; distillation is a full training run
Objective Next-token prediction on broad text Better task performance Better task performance on a specific task or domain Match human preferences (helpful, honest, harmless) Cheaper, faster inference
Expertise High Low Medium Medium to high Medium

Read the table across, not down. Moving right from prompting adds data requirements, training cost, and the risk of degrading general abilities (catastrophic forgetting). A lever is worth its cost only when the cheaper ones have been measured and fall short.

Current practice (October 2026). The time ranges are rough orders of magnitude; they depend on model size, data size, and hardware. Few teams pre-train their own LLM: they start from an open-weight or API model. Most alignment beyond the vendor’s own now uses DPO or similar offline methods (Chapter 4) rather than a full RLHF loop, which moves its expertise closer to that of fine-tuning.

Even a well-adapted model invents information when unsure and is unreliable at exact computation. It also knows nothing after its training cut-off. Production systems therefore add retrieval, tools, and verification around the model; see Chapter 5.

In short.

  • Scope, select, adapt and align, integrate, with evaluation throughout.
  • A narrow scope makes every later step easier.
  • Use the levers cheapest first: prompting, then fine-tuning, then alignment; pre-train only in rare cases.

1.8 Key takeaways

The section summaries above hold the details. The chapter in five lines:

  • What: an LLM predicts the next token; most text tasks reduce to that.
  • How: transformers replaced recurrence with attention, which is parallel, direct, and quadratic in length.
  • Shapes: encoder, decoder, and encoder–decoder models suit different tasks.
  • Steering: prompts and in-context examples shape what it does; generation settings shape how it samples.
  • Process: scope narrowly, start from an existing model, adapt cheapest first, and evaluate throughout.

1.9 Interview checkpoints

  • Why did transformers replace RNNs? Parallel training across positions, and direct attention between any two tokens instead of a long chain of recurrent steps.
  • Write the attention formula. Why the \(\sqrt{d_k}\)? \(\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V\). Without scaling, large dot products saturate the softmax and shrink the gradients.
  • What are Q, K, and V? Learned projections of each token: what it is looking for (query), what it offers to be matched on (key), and the content it passes on when attended to (value).
  • Why multi-head attention? Several heads can capture different relations in parallel at the same total cost.
  • Why does attention need positional information? Attention treats its inputs as an unordered set; positional encodings or RoPE inject word order.
  • What does causal masking do? It stops each decoder position from seeing future tokens, so training matches left-to-right generation.
  • What is the complexity of self-attention in sequence length? Quadratic, \(O(n^2)\).
  • Top-k vs. top-p? Top-k keeps a fixed number of tokens. Top-p keeps a variable number whose cumulative probability reaches \(p\).
  • What does temperature do? It divides the logits before the softmax. Lower values sharpen the distribution; higher values flatten it.
  • Zero-shot vs. few-shot? When do you move to fine-tuning? Few-shot includes solved examples in the prompt. Move on when a handful of examples does not reach the target performance, or when context or cost limits bite.
