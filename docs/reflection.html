Generative AI, Large Language Models, and Coding Agents

Chapter 8 Reflection

People rarely send the first draft of anything important. They reread it, notice that “next week” is ambiguous or that a figure is wrong, and fix it. Reflection gives an LLM workflow the same step. The model, or a second model, reviews an output and produces an improved version.

Reflection is the easiest agentic pattern to add to an existing system. It is also the easiest one to overrate. This chapter explains how it works, when it helps, why it often fails without outside information, and how to measure whether it is worth its cost.

8.1 The basic pattern

A reflection workflow has two or more LLM calls in sequence:

  1. Generate. Prompt the model for a first draft (version 1).
  2. Critique. Pass version 1 to a model with a different prompt. Ask it to review the draft against stated criteria and list the problems.
  3. Revise. Ask the model to write version 2 that fixes those problems.

Steps 2 and 3 are often combined into a single “review and rewrite” prompt. Keeping them separate makes the critique visible, which helps with debugging and evaluation.

The critique step does not always lead to a rewrite. It can also filter: generate twenty candidate taglines, then have the critic keep only the three that meet every criterion. Brainstorm-then-filter is often cheaper and safer than asking for one perfect answer.

Example. An analyst asks a model to draft a short client email summarizing a campaign’s results. The first draft says “performance improved last month” and quotes a click-through rate that does not match the report. A reflection prompt that says “check every number against the attached report and replace vague time references with dates” catches both problems. The revised draft names the month and uses the correct figure.

The same structure works for many outputs:

Output What the critique step looks for
Email or report Tone, accuracy of facts and promises, missing details, length
Code Bugs, unhandled edge cases, readability
Structured data (deeply nested JSON, HTML tables) Malformed structure, missing fields, wrong types
Step-by-step instructions Missing or out-of-order steps, ambiguity
Names, taglines, or brand copy Hard to pronounce, unintended meanings in the target markets’ languages
SQL query Wrong joins, wrong filters, missing date ranges

8.1.1 Same model or a different one?

The critic can be the same model with a different prompt, or a different model. Different models have different strengths. A common configuration drafts with a fast, cheap model and critiques with a reasoning model, because models that think before answering tend to be better at spotting bugs and logical gaps. Treat the choice of generator and critic as a configuration to test, not a fixed rule.

Background. Critique-and-revise also appears at training time. Constitutional AI (Bai, Kadavath, et al. 2022) has a model critique and revise its own responses against written principles, then trains on the revisions (Section 4.6). Reflection as discussed here happens at inference time and changes no weights.

In short.

  • Reflection is generate, critique, revise. The critique can also filter a set of candidates instead of rewriting one.
  • The critic can be the same model or a different one; test which works.
  • It happens at inference time; Constitutional AI uses the same idea at training time.

8.2 What the evidence says

Reflection is not magic. Its effect ranges from large to zero to negative, depending on the task and on whether the critic has new information.

Where self-feedback helps. Self-Refine (Madaan et al. 2023) used one model to generate, give feedback on, and refine its own output, with no extra training and no external tools. Across seven tasks (including dialogue responses, code optimization, code readability, and constrained generation), humans and automatic metrics preferred the refined outputs. Task performance rose by about 20 percentage points on average.

Where it does not. In the same paper, math reasoning barely moved: GPT-4 went from 92.9% to 93.1% on GSM8K, and GPT-3.5 did not improve at all. The authors traced this to the critic. A reasoning chain that looks consistent convinces the model that everything is fine. ChatGPT’s feedback was essentially “everything looks good” for 94% of the math problems. When an external signal told the model that its answer was wrong, the gains exceeded 5 points.

Huang et al. (2024) tested intrinsic self-correction, where the model reviews its own answer with no external feedback, on reasoning benchmarks. Performance did not improve and sometimes fell. For GPT-3.5 on GSM8K, accuracy went from 75.9% to 75.1% after one round of self-correction and to 74.7% after two, because the model changed some correct answers to wrong ones. They also showed that some earlier reports of self-correction gains had used ground-truth labels to decide when to stop, which a deployed system does not have.

Where external feedback makes the difference. Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023) has an agent reflect in words on task feedback, such as the results of unit tests, and store the reflections in memory for its next attempt. It reached 91% pass@1 on HumanEval, against 80% for GPT-4 used directly. CRITIC (Gou et al. 2024) has the model check its output with tools (search for facts, a code interpreter for programs) before revising. It improved question answering, mathematical program synthesis, and toxicity reduction, and its authors stress that the external feedback is what matters.

Why the pattern looks like this. A critic that sees only the draft has the same knowledge and blind spots as the model that wrote it. It catches problems that are easier to recognize than to avoid, such as a missing sign-off, a wrong format, a forgotten requirement, or awkward phrasing. It tends to miss subtle reasoning errors, and it may “fix” correct answers to please the reviewer prompt. Models trained on human preferences are prone to this kind of agreeable over-correction (Sharma et al. 2024). New information breaks the symmetry: the critic now knows something the generator did not.

The studies side by side:

Study Feedback the critic gets Result
Self-Refine (Madaan et al. 2023) None (self-feedback) About +20 points on average across seven tasks; almost nothing on math
Huang et al. (Huang et al. 2024) None (intrinsic self-correction) No gain on reasoning; GPT-3.5 on GSM8K fell slightly
Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023) Unit-test results, kept in memory 91% pass@1 on HumanEval vs. 80% for GPT-4 alone
CRITIC (Gou et al. 2024) Search results, code interpreter Gains on QA, math programs, and toxicity reduction

The pattern: the more outside information the critic has, the more reliable the gain.

In short.

  • Self-critique helps on writing, code style, and format, where problems are easy to recognize.
  • It fails on reasoning, and can turn right answers wrong, when the critic has nothing new to go on.
  • External feedback is what makes the gains consistent.

8.3 Reflection with external feedback

External feedback means information from outside the model that is added to the critique step. It is the single most effective way to make reflection work. Typical sources:

Source What it reveals Example
Code execution Errors, stack traces, wrong outputs Run the generated script; pass the traceback to the critic
Unit tests Which behaviors fail Return failing test names and their assertion messages
Validators Schema or format violations A JSON-schema validator reports missing fields
Rule checks in code Policy violations A regular expression finds competitor names in a draft reply
Counters Length limits Count the words and report “212 words, limit is 150”
Search or retrieval Facts the draft got wrong Retrieve a source and pass the relevant passage to the critic
Database execution Empty, huge, or failed results Run the SQL query and report the row count or error
A human Anything a person notices A reviewer marks a paragraph as off-brand

Two of these deserve a comment.

  • Length. LLMs are poor at hitting exact word counts, because they see tokens, not words. A few lines of code that count the words and report the overshoot fix this more reliably than any amount of prompt tuning.
  • Fact checking. Suppose a draft states that BLEU and ROUGE were both introduced in 2002. A retrieval step finds that BLEU dates from 2002 (Papineni et al. 2002) and ROUGE from 2004 (Lin 2004). Passing those passages to the critic turns a plausible error into a specific, fixable one.

Example: the external-feedback loop in code. The loop below writes a SQL query, runs it, and feeds any error or suspicious result back to the model. It stops when the checks pass or after three rounds. The helper names are illustrative.

def answer_with_sql(question, schema, max_rounds=3):
    query = llm(f"Schema:\n{schema}\n\nWrite one SQL query to answer: {question}")
    for _ in range(max_rounds):
        result = run_sql(query)                 # tool: the outside world
        problems = check(result)                # code, not the model's opinion
        if not problems:
            return query, result                # stop: objective checks pass
        query = llm(
            f"Question: {question}\nQuery:\n{query}\n"
            f"Problems found when running it: {problems}\n"
            "Return a corrected query only."
        )
    return query, None                          # stop: round limit, flag for review

def check(result):
    if result.error:
        return f"Database error: {result.error}"
    if result.row_count == 0:
        return "The query returned no rows. Check filters and date ranges."
    return None

Three things make this work. The feedback is specific (the actual error message). The stop condition is checked by code, not by asking the model if it is done. And the loop has a hard limit, with a failure path that a human can pick up.

In short.

  • External feedback is information from outside the model: execution results, tests, validators, counters, retrieval, or a human.
  • It is the most effective way to make reflection work.
  • When prompt tuning plateaus, ask what new information the system could get about its own output.

8.3.1 A practitioner’s view of the improvement curve

A pattern many practitioners report, and a useful mental model rather than a measured law:

  • Tuning a single prompt improves quality quickly at first, then plateaus.
  • Adding a reflection step and tuning its prompt often lifts the plateau somewhat.
  • Adding external feedback to the reflection step often lifts it further, and keeps improving as the feedback becomes more informative.

So when prompt tuning is showing diminishing returns, ask: what new information could the system obtain about its own output? That question usually points to the next improvement.

8.4 Multimodal reflection: charts and images

Reflection is not limited to text. A charting workflow can:

  1. ask a model to write plotting code for a request such as “compare weekly spend and conversions by channel for Q1”;
  2. run the code to render the chart as an image;
  3. give the code, the image, and the original request to a multimodal model and ask it to critique the chart for readability, clarity, and whether it answers the question; and
  4. ask for revised code and render again.

The rendered image is a form of external feedback. The critic sees what the reader will see: overlapping labels, a stacked bar chart that makes comparisons hard, a missing axis title, or a legend that hides the data. A first draft might produce stacked bars that bury the comparison. A good critique recommends grouped bars or a two-panel line chart, and the revision implements it.

Rendering also catches errors that reading the code would not, such as an empty plot caused by a wrong column name.

In short.

  • A multimodal critic can review a rendered chart, not just the code.
  • The image is external feedback: the critic sees what the reader will see.

8.5 Writing reflection prompts

A reflection prompt is an ordinary prompt (Chapter 6) with a specific job. What makes it work:

  • Say explicitly that the input is a draft to review, and what it was supposed to achieve. Include the original request.
  • Give concrete criteria. “Check the tone, verify every date and figure against the provided facts, and confirm the call to action is present” works better than “improve this”.
  • Supply the reference material the draft should be consistent with: the facts, the data, the style guide, the tool outputs.
  • Separate critique from revision when you want to inspect or evaluate the critique, or when a different model should do each part.
  • Allow “no changes needed”. A critic forced to find problems will invent them.
  • Ask for structured critique (a list of issues, each with location and fix) when code will act on it, for example to decide whether to run another round.

An illustrative critique prompt:

You are reviewing a draft client email written by a junior analyst.

<request>{original_request}</request>
<facts>{campaign_metrics_and_dates}</facts>
<draft>{draft_email}</draft>

Check the draft against these criteria:
1. Every number and date matches <facts>.
2. No promise is made that <facts> does not support.
3. The tone is professional and concise (under 150 words).
4. It ends with a clear next step.

List each problem with the sentence it occurs in and a suggested fix.
If there are no problems, reply exactly: NO_ISSUES.

A practical way to learn to write better prompts, reflection prompts included, is to read prompts from well-regarded open-source agent projects.

In short.

  • Say it is a draft, include the original request, and give concrete criteria and reference material.
  • Allow “no changes needed”, or the critic will invent problems.
  • Ask for structured critique when code will act on it.

8.6 Design choices and costs

Choice Options Guidance
Critic model Same model; a stronger model; a reasoning model Test combinations on your eval set
Rounds One; a fixed maximum; until a stop condition One or two rounds capture most of the gain; always cap the loop
Stop condition Critic says no issues; all checks pass; max rounds Prefer objective checks (tests pass, schema valid) over the critic’s opinion
Feedback None; tool results; human Add external feedback wherever it is cheap to get
Scope Whole output; only the parts that failed a check Targeted revision reduces drift and cost

Reflection adds at least one model call per round, so it increases latency and cost. It can also make things worse: a revision can drift away from constraints the first draft satisfied, or change a correct answer. Both are reasons to measure it rather than assume it helps.

In short.

  • One or two rounds capture most of the gain; always cap the loop.
  • Stop on objective checks where possible, not on the critic’s opinion.
  • Reflection costs latency and money and can make outputs worse, so measure it.

8.7 Measuring whether reflection helps

Before keeping a reflection step, compare the workflow with and without it on the same inputs. Chapter 10 develops these ideas fully; here is the version specific to reflection.

Objective tasks: compare against ground truth. Suppose a model writes SQL to answer questions about a retail database, and a reflection step reviews each query before it runs. Collect a set of questions with known answers, such as “How many units were sold in May?” or “Which product color has the highest revenue?”. Run both versions and count how often each returns the correct answer.

Two cautions from basic statistics:

  • Small sets are noisy. With 15 questions, one question is 6.7 percentage points. A jump from 13/15 to 14/15 correct is one question and could easily be chance. Grow the set as the system matures.
  • Compare paired outcomes. Both versions answer the same questions, so look at the questions where they disagree. If reflection fixes five answers and breaks one, that is more informative than the two accuracy figures alone. McNemar’s test formalizes this comparison.

For example, with 40 questions:

Reflection correct Reflection wrong
No reflection correct 30 1
No reflection wrong 5 4

Accuracy goes from 31/40 to 35/40. The informative cells are the disagreements: reflection fixed 5 answers and broke 1. Read those six cases. They show what the reflection step catches, and what it damages.

The same eval set then lets you compare alternative generation and reflection prompts quickly, instead of guessing.

Subjective tasks: use a rubric, not a pairwise vote. For charts or prose there is no single right answer, so an LLM-as-a-judge is common. The obvious design, showing the judge both outputs and asking which is better, has known problems. Judges are sensitive to prompt wording, may disagree with human experts, and show position bias. In Zheng et al. (2023), Claude-v1 favored whichever answer came first in 75% of swapped pairs under the default prompt, and GPT-3.5 in 50%. GPT-4 was the most consistent judge but still favored the first answer in 30% of pairs. The direction is not fixed: with a reworded prompt, Claude-v1 leaned toward the second answer instead. If you must compare pairs, run each comparison in both orders and count only consistent verdicts.

A more stable design grades each output separately against a rubric of specific yes/no criteria. For a chart:

  • Does it have a clear, descriptive title?
  • Are both axes labeled, with units?
  • Is the chart type appropriate for the comparison requested?
  • Are the axis ranges sensible (no truncated axis exaggerating a change)?
  • Can every series be distinguished without relying on color alone?
  • Does it answer the question that was asked?

Sum the passes to get a score. In code, the judge returns one 0 or 1 per criterion as JSON, so the scores can be added up and compared:

RUBRIC = ["clear_title", "axes_labeled_with_units", "appropriate_chart_type",
          "sensible_axis_ranges", "series_distinguishable", "answers_question"]

JUDGE_PROMPT = """Grade this chart against each criterion. Score 1 if it fully
meets the criterion, otherwise 0.
Request: {request}
Criteria: {criteria}
Return JSON only, for example {{"clear_title": 1, "axes_labeled_with_units": 0, ...}}"""

def rubric_score(request, chart_image):
    raw = judge_llm(JUDGE_PROMPT.format(request=request, criteria=RUBRIC),
                    image=chart_image)
    scores = json.loads(raw)
    return sum(scores[c] for c in RUBRIC)          # 0 to 6

with_refl = [rubric_score(r, make_chart(r, reflect=True))  for r in requests]
without   = [rubric_score(r, make_chart(r, reflect=False)) for r in requests]
print(mean(with_refl), mean(without))

Each chart is graded on its own, so position bias does not arise. The per-criterion scores also show which criterion reflection improves. Practitioners widely report that several binary criteria give more consistent judgments than asking for a single 1–5 rating, which models tend to calibrate poorly. Treat this as practitioner guidance; it is consistent with the evidence on judge instability, but it is not a settled research result. Check the judge against a sample of human ratings before trusting it.

Rule of thumb. When a criterion is objective, check it with code. Use an LLM judge only for what code cannot check, and expect to spend time tuning the rubric.

In short.

  • Compare with and without reflection on the same inputs, and read the cases where they disagree.
  • Objective tasks: score against ground truth. Subjective tasks: grade each output against binary rubric items, not pairwise.
  • Small eval sets are noisy; one question out of 15 is almost 7 points.

8.8 Key takeaways

The section summaries above hold the details. The chapter in five lines:

  • What: generate, critique, revise (or filter), at inference time.
  • When it works: when problems are easy to recognize, and above all when the critic gets external feedback such as test results or retrieved facts.
  • When it fails: self-critique on reasoning with nothing new to go on.
  • How to build it: concrete criteria, reference material, “no changes” allowed, objective stop conditions, a hard round limit.
  • How to keep it honest: measure with and without it on the same inputs, and judge subjective output with binary rubrics.

8.9 Interview checkpoints

  • What is the reflection pattern? Generate a draft, critique it against criteria, and revise. Same or different model, one or more rounds.
  • Does self-reflection always improve output? No. Self-Refine reported large average gains, but almost none on math reasoning. Huang et al. found that intrinsic self-correction did not improve, and sometimes hurt, reasoning accuracy.
  • Why does external feedback help so much? A critic with only the draft shares the generator’s knowledge and blind spots. Test results, error messages, or retrieved facts give it information the generator lacked.
  • Give three cheap sources of external feedback. Running the code, a schema validator, a regular-expression check, a word counter, a database query’s row count or error, a retrieval-based fact check.
  • Your prompt tuning has plateaued. What do you try next? Add a reflection step, and better still, find external feedback the system can gather about its own output.
  • Why might you use a different model for the critique? Models differ in strengths. Reasoning models are often better at finding bugs and logical gaps than at fast drafting.
  • How do you decide whether to keep a reflection step? Run the workflow with and without it on the same labeled inputs. Compare accuracy, look at which examples changed, and weigh the gain against the extra latency and cost.
  • Why is “which of these two outputs is better?” a weak LLM-judge design? Position bias and prompt sensitivity. Grade each output against a rubric of binary criteria instead, or at least evaluate both orders.
  • How do you stop a reflection loop? When objective checks pass, when the critic reports no issues, or at a maximum number of rounds, whichever comes first.
  • How does Constitutional AI relate to reflection? It uses the same critique-and-revise idea, but at training time to produce training data. Reflection in a workflow happens at inference time and changes no weights.
