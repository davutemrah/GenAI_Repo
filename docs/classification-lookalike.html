Generative AI, Large Language Models, and Coding Agents

Chapter 16 Classification and Lookalike Modeling: PU Learning, Two-Tower Networks, and Typed Decision Models

16.1 Introduction

Marketing data science classifies constantly. It scores leads, routes inquiries, labels reviews, and finds new customers who resemble existing ones. Three developments have changed how this work can be done:

  1. Positive-unlabeled (PU) learning gives lookalike modeling a principled footing when advertisers know their positives (the seed audience) but have no confirmed negatives.
  2. Two-tower neural networks embed users and items in a shared vector space so that similarity search scales to hundreds of millions of users.
  3. Typed decision models, such as TypeSafe AI’s Jev (released in September 2026), return probabilities for predefined choices instead of generating text. They target fast, cheap, structured classification that generative LLMs handle awkwardly.

This chapter explains when each approach fits, what it assumes, and how to validate it before it touches live campaigns. The interactive Classification Lab lets you experiment with the confidence formulas, calibration, and PU audience sizing used in this chapter. It builds on the transformer background in Chapter 1 and the workflow chapters on notebooks (Chapter 12), verification loops (Chapter 11), and MCP (Chapter 13).

Provenance note. This chapter began as an AI-generated research summary. Its claims were checked against primary sources. Several were corrected or removed because they could not be verified, or because they confused a vendor’s marketing claims with established results. Vendor claims that remain are labeled [Vendor].

16.2 Choosing a model family

Approach Core mechanism Typical marketing use Main assumption or risk
Classical supervised ML (logistic regression, gradient boosting) Learns a decision boundary from labeled features Propensity, churn, lead scoring with reliable labels Labels represent the target; no leakage
PU learning Learns from positives plus unlabeled records Lookalike audiences, account expansion How positives were selected into the seed (often assumed random)
Two-tower networks Separate user and item encoders, compared by dot product Large-scale retrieval, recommendations, embedding-based lookalikes Enough interaction data; well-designed negative sampling
Generative LLMs Generate text token by token Labeling ambiguous text, explanations, content Cost, latency, output-format errors, ungrounded claims
Typed decision models (e.g., Jev) Return probabilities over a predefined schema High-volume routing, triage, text-to-feature labeling, guardrails Vendor-reported accuracy and calibration must be checked on your data

None of these is a causal method. A high lookalike score or propensity score says who resembles converters, not who would convert because of the campaign. Incrementality needs an experiment or a credible quasi-experimental design.

16.3 Lookalike modeling as positive-unlabeled learning

16.3.1 The problem: no true negatives

A lookalike model starts from a seed audience, such as repeat purchasers or high-value accounts, and searches a wider population for similar people. The wider population is not negative. It is unlabeled: it contains future customers, customers of competitors, and people who simply were never observed.

Treating all unlabeled records as negatives is the common shortcut. It biases probability estimates downward because the “negatives” contain hidden positives.

16.3.2 The key assumption and a useful result

Most PU methods assume that labeled positives are a random sample of all positives, often called selected completely at random (SCAR). Under SCAR, Elkan and Noto (2008) showed that:

\[ P(y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{P(s = 1 \mid x)}{c}, \qquad c = P(s = 1 \mid y = 1), \]

where \(s = 1\) means “in the seed” and \(y = 1\) means “truly positive.” Two practical consequences follow:

  1. Ranking is unchanged. Dividing by a constant does not change the order of scores. If you only need the top-k users, the naive seed-versus-rest classifier already produces the same ranking as the PU-corrected one under SCAR.
  2. Probabilities and audience sizes change. If you need calibrated probabilities, expected conversions, or an estimate of how many positives exist in the population, you must estimate \(c\).

The SCAR assumption is often doubtful in marketing. Seed audiences come from past campaigns, specific channels, or loyalty programs, so they are rarely a random sample of all potential customers. When selection depends on features, the naive ranking inherits that selection bias. More recent methods relax SCAR; see the survey by Bekker and Davis (2020).

16.3.3 Common strategies

  • Two-step methods first identify reliable negatives in the unlabeled data and then train an ordinary classifier.
    • Spy technique: hide a random subset of seed members (“spies”) in the unlabeled set, train seed versus unlabeled, and treat unlabeled records that score below most spies as reliable negatives (Liu et al., 2002).
    • Initial-classifier approach: train seed versus unlabeled, then take the lowest-scoring unlabeled records as reliable negatives.
  • Bagging PU trains many classifiers, each on the positives against a random subsample of unlabeled records, and averages their scores (Mordelet and Vert, 2014).
  • Class-prior methods estimate \(c\) (or the positive share) and correct the classifier’s output, as in Elkan and Noto.

16.3.4 Worked example: a retailer’s lookalike audience

The numbers are invented and kept round so the logic is easy to follow.

Setup. A retailer’s addressable population has 1,000,000 people. Suppose (unknown to the analyst) 200,000 of them would become high-value customers. The seed audience is 60,000 known high-value customers. So the seed captures \(c = 60{,}000 / 200{,}000 = 0.30\) of all true positives.

Step 1: the naive model. The analyst trains “seed versus everyone else.” The model outputs \(P(s = 1 \mid x)\): the probability that a person is in the seed, not the probability that they are high value.

Person Naive score \(P(s=1 \mid x)\) Corrected \(P(y=1 \mid x) = \text{score} / 0.30\)
Ana 0.15 0.50
Ben 0.06 0.20
Cem 0.01 0.03

What changes and what does not.

  • Ranking is the same. Ana > Ben > Cem under both columns. If the media plan says “target the top 100,000 people,” the naive model already picks the right people (under SCAR).
  • Probabilities and sizes change. The naive scores sum to roughly the seed size (about 60,000) across the population, so the naive model implies “about 60,000 people like our customers exist.” The corrected scores sum to about 200,000. If a client asks “how big is the opportunity?” or “how many conversions should we expect?”, the naive answer is too low by a factor of about three.

Step 2: the spy technique in numbers. To find reliable negatives, hide 6,000 seed members (10%) as “spies” inside the unlabeled population and train seed versus unlabeled. Suppose 95% of spies score above 0.08. Unlabeled people scoring below 0.08 look less like customers than almost every real customer does, so they are treated as reliable negatives. A second model is then trained on seed versus those reliable negatives.

Step 3: when the assumption fails. Suppose the seed came only from the email loyalty program. Mobile-app-only customers are high value but rarely in the program, so they are under-represented in the seed. The model learns “looks like an email subscriber” rather than “looks like a high-value customer.” It ranks valuable mobile-only prospects low, and no PU correction fixes that, because the correction assumes the seed is a random sample of all positives. The remedies are a better seed (combine sources), features that describe selection, or methods that relax SCAR.

To see how class overlap distorts audience sizing while leaving the ranking unchanged, open tab 3 of the Classification Lab.

16.3.5 A runnable illustration

The simulation below knows the true labels, so it can show both what the Elkan–Noto correction does and when it fails. Results were produced with scikit-learn 1.5; other versions may differ slightly.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split


def pu_demo(class_sep: float, label_freq: float = 0.3, seed: int = 42) -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20_000, n_features=10, n_informative=5,
                               weights=[0.8, 0.2], class_sep=class_sep,
                               random_state=seed)
    # Seed audience: a random 30% of true positives (the SCAR assumption)
    s = ((y == 1) & (rng.random(len(y)) < label_freq)).astype(int)

    X_fit, X_rest, s_fit, s_rest, _, y_rest = train_test_split(
        X, s, y, test_size=0.4, random_state=seed, stratify=s)
    X_hold, X_te, s_hold, _, _, y_te = train_test_split(
        X_rest, s_rest, y_rest, test_size=0.5, random_state=seed, stratify=s_rest)

    # Naive model: seed (s=1) vs everyone else treated as negative
    clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X_fit, s_fit)

    # Elkan-Noto: c = P(s=1 | y=1), estimated on held-out seed members
    c_hat = clf.predict_proba(X_hold[s_hold == 1])[:, 1].mean()
    p_pos = np.clip(clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1] / c_hat, 0, 1)

    print(f"class_sep={class_sep}: c_hat={c_hat:.2f} (true {label_freq}), "
          f"AUC={roc_auc_score(y_te, p_pos):.3f}, "
          f"est. positive share={p_pos.mean():.3f} (true {y_te.mean():.3f})")


for sep in [1.0, 3.0]:
    pu_demo(sep)
class_sep=1.0: c_hat=0.11 (true 0.3), AUC=0.832, est. positive share=0.465 (true 0.207)
class_sep=3.0: c_hat=0.24 (true 0.3), AUC=0.981, est. positive share=0.209 (true 0.207)

Interpretation.

  • When positives and negatives are well separated (class_sep=3.0), the estimate of \(c\) is close to the truth and the estimated positive share is accurate.
  • When the classes overlap (class_sep=1.0), which is typical of behavioral marketing data, the estimator of \(c\) is badly biased downward. The positive share is then overstated by more than a factor of two. This estimator assumes the classifier gives true positives a score near \(c\), which fails when classes overlap.
  • The ranking (AUC) is identical to the naive classifier in both cases, illustrating consequence 1 above.

Takeaway: use PU corrections for audience sizing only after checking their assumptions. For audience ranking, the larger risks are selection bias in the seed and leakage, not the missing negatives.

16.4 Two-tower networks for retrieval and lookalikes

16.4.1 Architecture

A two-tower model has two independent neural networks:

  • the user tower encodes user features (demographics, context, recent behavior, engagement history) into an embedding vector \(u\); and
  • the item tower encodes item features (category, text description, images, popularity) into an embedding \(v\) of the same dimension.

The model scores a user–item pair by their similarity:

\[ \text{score}(u, v) = u \cdot v = \sum_{i=1}^{d} u_i v_i \quad \text{or} \quad \cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\lVert u \rVert \, \lVert v \rVert}. \]

The dot product rewards both direction and magnitude. Cosine similarity uses only direction, which removes the effect of vector length, often a proxy for popularity.

The idea goes back to the deep structured semantic model for search (Huang et al., 2013). YouTube’s candidate-generation network (Covington et al., 2016) and its sampling-bias-corrected two-tower model (Yi et al., 2019) made it standard for large-scale recommendation.

16.4.2 Why it scales: late interaction and approximate search

The towers interact only at the final similarity step (“late fusion”). That separation enables a two-stage system:

  1. Offline: compute embeddings for every item (or every user) and store them in a vector index.
  2. Online: compute the query embedding and run an approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search to retrieve the top candidates quickly, even across very large catalogs.

Because the towers never see each other’s features before the final step, they cannot model detailed user–item feature crosses. Production systems therefore add a ranking stage. A heavier model, such as Wide & Deep (Cheng et al., 2016) or DeepFM (Guo et al., 2017), re-scores the few hundred retrieved candidates and applies business rules such as diversity, frequency caps, and brand safety.

16.4.3 Using embeddings for lookalike audiences

Once user embeddings exist, lookalike modeling becomes a search problem:

  • Centroid search: average the seed users’ embeddings and retrieve the nearest non-seed users. This is simple but assumes a single, compact seed cluster.
  • Multi-anchor search: cluster the seed first, then search around each cluster center. This works better when the seed contains distinct personas.
  • Classifier in embedding space: train a PU or ordinary classifier on the embeddings, combining the two approaches in this chapter.

16.4.4 Worked example: dot product versus cosine

Real embeddings have dozens to hundreds of dimensions that have no names. For intuition, imagine three dimensions: sport, tech, and family.

A user who mostly buys sports gear, sometimes family items, and rarely tech has the embedding \(u = (0.9, 0.1, 0.4)\). Three candidate items:

Item Embedding \(v\) Length \(\lVert v \rVert\) Dot product \(u \cdot v\) Cosine
Running shoes (0.8, 0.0, 0.3) 0.85 0.84 0.99
Phone case (very popular) (1.5, 1.2, 1.0) 2.17 1.87 0.87
Kids’ bike (0.2, 0.1, 0.9) 0.93 0.55 0.60

How to read it:

  • The running shoes point in almost exactly the user’s direction (cosine 0.99): they are the best match in taste.
  • The phone case has a long vector. In many trained models, popular items end up with larger embeddings because they appear in many interactions. The dot product rewards that length, so the phone case wins on dot product even though it matches the user’s taste less well.
  • Neither metric is “right.” Dot product mixes relevance with popularity, which can raise click-through but narrows exposure. Cosine focuses on taste, which helps niche items and new products. Choose deliberately and evaluate both.

16.4.5 Worked example: why a single centroid can fail

Suppose the seed has two personas in a two-dimensional embedding: bargain hunters near (1, 0) and premium buyers near (0, 1). Their centroid is (0.5, 0.5).

Prospect Distance to centroid Distance to nearest persona
Bargain-hunter-like (0.9, 0.1) 0.57 0.14
Premium-like (0.1, 0.9) 0.57 0.14
In-between (0.5, 0.5) 0.00 0.71

A centroid search ranks the in-between prospect first even though that person resembles neither real persona. Clustering the seed first and searching around each cluster center (multi-anchor search) ranks the two persona-like prospects first, which is what the client wants.

16.4.6 Training choices that matter

  • Negative sampling. Interaction logs contain mostly positives (clicks, purchases). Negatives usually come from in-batch sampling or random sampling. In-batch sampling over-represents popular items, which Yi et al. correct for. The ratio of negatives to positives is a tuning choice, not a fixed rule.
  • Popularity bias. Without correction, retrieval favors already-popular items and users who resemble the majority. Common responses are sampling corrections, re-weighting rare items, and diversity rules at the ranking stage.
  • Cold start. Because the towers use content and context features, new users and items get embeddings from their metadata. Their quality still needs separate evaluation.
  • Privacy. User embeddings derived from behavior are personal data in many jurisdictions. Govern them like the raw data.

On Databricks, embeddings are typically stored in Delta tables and served through AI Search (formerly Vector Search) for ANN retrieval. Check the current documentation for supported index types and limits.

16.5 Typed decision models: Jev and “System One” classification

16.5.1 Why a different model type

Generative LLMs can classify text, but for high-volume pipelines they bring three costs:

  • latency and token cost, because they generate output token by token;
  • format errors when asked to return JSON; and
  • free-text output that downstream code must parse and validate.

Teams already address these with structured-output features, small fine-tuned encoders (BERT-style classifiers), or embedding-plus-classifier pipelines. A newer option is a model built only for typed decisions.

16.5.2 What Jev is

TypeSafe AI describes Jev as the first “System One model,” a name borrowed from Kahneman’s distinction between fast, intuitive thinking and slow, deliberate thinking (TypeSafe announcement, Sep 2026). Jev takes a piece of state (text or JSON) and one or more typed questions, and returns only structured answers. It never generates prose, explanations, or code.

According to the TypeSafe documentation, it supports three question primitives:

Primitive Question form Returns Marketing example
Choice Pick one option from a list The chosen option, a probability for each option, confidence Route an inquiry to billing, support, or sales
Score Place the state on an ordered rubric A score, probabilities over levels, confidence Rate lead urgency or customer frustration
Noul Is this statement true? A single probability that the answer is yes “Does this email request a refund?”

TypeSafe says it trains Jev with a method it calls Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), which it contrasts with the RLHF used for chat models. It has not published the method’s details.

16.5.3 Confidence is not calibration

TypeSafe’s documentation defines confidence as a summary of how concentrated the probability distribution is (TypeSafe: Confidence):

  • Choice, with \(n\) options and top probability \(p_{\max}\): \[\text{confidence} = \frac{p_{\max} - 1/n}{1 - 1/n},\] which is 0 for an even split and 1 for certainty.
  • Noul, with probability \(p\): the suggested summary is \(\text{confidence} = |2p - 1|\).
  • Score, with level probabilities \(p_i\) and most likely level \(m\): \[\text{confidence} = \max\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{\sum_i p_i \lvert i - m \rvert}{\text{MAD}_{\text{unif}}}\right),\] where \(\text{MAD}_{\text{unif}}\) is the same average distance for an even spread across all levels, measured from the middle level. Uncertainty between adjacent levels lowers confidence less than uncertainty between distant levels.

16.5.4 Worked examples: computing confidence by hand

Choice: routing a customer email to one of four teams (\(n = 4\), so an even split gives each team \(1/n = 0.25\)).

Returned probabilities (Billing, Support, Sales, Cancel) \(p_{\max}\) Confidence
0.70, 0.15, 0.10, 0.05 0.70 \((0.70 - 0.25)/(1 - 0.25) = 0.60\)
0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10 0.40 \((0.40 - 0.25)/(0.75) = 0.20\)
0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25 0.25 0.00

Read confidence as “how far the top answer stands above a coin-toss among the options,” rescaled to run from 0 to 1.

Score: rating complaint urgency on five levels numbered 0 (calm) to 4 (furious). For five levels, an even spread has an average distance of 1.2 levels from the middle level, so \(\text{MAD}_{\text{unif}} = 1.2\).

Case Probabilities on levels 0–4 Most likely level Average distance \(\sum p_i \lvert i - m \rvert\) Confidence
A: torn between angry and furious 0, 0, 0, 0.6, 0.4 3 \(0.4 \times 1 = 0.4\) \(1 - 0.4/1.2 = 0.67\)
B: torn between angry and calm 0.4, 0, 0, 0.6, 0 3 \(0.4 \times 3 = 1.2\) \(1 - 1.2/1.2 = 0.00\)

Both cases put 60% on level 3, yet A has useful confidence and B has none. In A the model only hesitates about how angry the customer is. In B it cannot tell whether the customer is angry or calm, which is useless for prioritizing.

Noul: “Does this email request a refund?”

Returned \(p\) Confidence \(\lvert 2p - 1 \rvert\) Reading
0.94 0.88 Confident yes
0.55 0.10 Close to a coin toss
0.06 0.88 Confident no

Confidently wrong. In the first Choice row, suppose the email actually came from a customer about to cancel. The model said Billing with confidence 0.60, and it was wrong. Nothing in the formula can reveal that; only comparing predictions with true labels across many emails can. That comparison is calibration, described next.

Try your own distributions in tab 1 of the Classification Lab.

These formulas describe the shape of the distribution, not whether it is right. A model can be confidently wrong. Calibration means that among cases predicted at 90%, about 90% are actually positive. Calibration is an empirical property that you must measure on your own labeled data. TypeSafe says Jev is trained for calibrated probabilities, but no calibration evaluation (such as reliability diagrams or expected calibration error) was found in its published materials as of October 2026.

16.5.5 Vendor-reported performance

TypeSafe reports [Vendor]:

  • end-to-end latency of roughly 70 to 500 milliseconds, against seconds for frontier LLMs;
  • input pricing of $0.042 per million tokens, with output not billed; and
  • that answers always match the declared schema, so type errors are impossible by construction.

TypeSafe notes that its published evaluations were run from its own machines and that its workflow benchmarks were built by its own team. Schema conformance by construction is credible. Accuracy, calibration, and real-world latency still need independent testing. As of October 2026 the model was proprietary and served through TypeSafe’s hosted API; access terms may change.

16.5.6 Where a typed decision model fits

  • Routing and triage: a Choice question picks the destination while a Score question rates urgency, evaluated on the same input.
  • Text-to-feature labeling: turn reviews, survey open-ends, or call notes into categorical features for downstream models.
  • Guardrails for agents: ask a Noul question such as “Is this proposed action read-only?” before an agent runs a tool. This complements deterministic permissions and hooks; it does not replace them.

Design questions atomically: ask one factor per question, and let ordinary code combine the answers into the final action. This keeps each probability interpretable and testable.

16.6 Validating classifiers before automation

Whatever the model, decide thresholds from evidence, not from reported confidence.

  1. Build a labeled sample that represents production traffic, including rare and ambiguous cases. Have humans label it, and measure agreement between labelers.
  2. Run in shadow mode. Score live traffic in parallel with the current process without affecting decisions.
  3. Measure discrimination and calibration separately. Discrimination (AUC, precision at the chosen threshold) says whether the model ranks well. Calibration says whether its probabilities can be taken at face value.
  4. Set thresholds from costs. Choose automation thresholds from the cost of each error type, and route the uncertain middle band to people or to a slower model.
  5. Monitor drift. Re-check calibration when products, campaigns, or language change.

A minimal calibration check:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.calibration import calibration_curve
from sklearn.metrics import brier_score_loss


def calibration_report(y_true, p_pred, n_bins: int = 10) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Compare predicted probabilities with observed frequencies on labeled data."""
    frac_pos, mean_pred = calibration_curve(y_true, p_pred, n_bins=n_bins,
                                            strategy="quantile")
    print(f"Brier score: {brier_score_loss(y_true, p_pred):.3f}")
    return pd.DataFrame({"mean_predicted": mean_pred.round(2),
                         "observed_rate": frac_pos.round(2)})


# Simulated shadow-mode log: an overconfident classifier
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
true_p = rng.uniform(0, 1, 2_000)
y = rng.binomial(1, true_p)
p_reported = np.clip(0.5 + 1.6 * (true_p - 0.5), 0.01, 0.99)  # pushed toward 0/1

print(calibration_report(y, p_reported, n_bins=5))
Brier score: 0.180
   mean_predicted  observed_rate
0            0.01           0.10
1            0.18           0.30
2            0.49           0.51
3            0.82           0.68
4            0.99           0.91

The simulated model reports 0.99 when the observed rate is 0.91, and 0.01 when it is 0.10. A threshold of “automate above 0.95” would therefore be much less safe than it appears. If calibration is poor but ranking is good, recalibrate with Platt scaling or isotonic regression on held-out data. Do not tune the threshold on the same sample you use to report performance.

16.6.1 Worked example: from calibration to an automation threshold

Checking calibration in plain terms. In shadow mode, a classifier flags 1,000 emails as “refund request” with probability around 0.90. Human reviewers check them and find that 760 really are refund requests. The observed rate is 0.76, not 0.90, so the model is overconfident in that range. Repeat the check for other probability bands (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, …) to draw the full calibration curve.

Choosing a threshold from costs. Suppose the app escalates likely churn emails to a retention specialist:

  • escalating an email that was not churn risk (false positive) wastes about $1 of specialist time;
  • missing a real churn-risk email (false negative) loses about $9 in expected customer value.

With calibrated probability \(p\), escalating costs \((1 - p) \times 1\) in expectation and not escalating costs \(p \times 9\). Escalation is worth it when \(p \times 9 > (1 - p) \times 1\), that is, when

\[ p > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} = \frac{1}{1 + 9} = 0.10. \]

Two lessons follow:

  1. The threshold comes from the business costs, not from the model. Here escalating at only 10% probability is rational because misses are nine times as costly as false alarms. A default threshold of 0.5 would miss many saveable customers.
  2. The rule only works with calibrated probabilities. If the model’s “0.10” really means 0.03, the threshold is wrong. Calibrate first, then apply the cost rule.

Tab 2 of the Classification Lab simulates an overconfident model, draws its calibration curve, and converts business costs into a threshold.

For lookalike audiences, the final test is an outcome experiment: compare conversion or incremental lift for the new audience against a control or the current method. Offline metrics show the model learned the seed; only an experiment shows business value.

16.7 Putting the pieces together

A practical marketing stack might combine these approaches:

  • Two-tower embeddings for large-scale audience retrieval and recommendations.
  • PU-aware classifiers to rank and size lookalike audiences, with explicit checks on seed selection.
  • A typed decision model or small fine-tuned classifier for high-volume routing and text-to-feature labeling.
  • Generative LLMs for explanation, ambiguous cases, and content, connected to governed tools through MCP (Chapter 13).
  • Verification loops around all of them: execution checks, quality checks against labeled data, and outcome experiments (Chapter 11).

Develop these models in reproducible notebooks with a clear setup, analysis, and outputs structure, deterministic seeds, and held-out evaluation, as described in Chapter 12.

16.8 Implementing this in a Databricks marketing practice

This section turns the chapter into four concrete projects for an agency data science team that runs reusable Databricks jobs and Streamlit apps. Each project lists the steps, the deliverable, and how to prove it works. Product names follow the Databricks documentation as of October 2026; check current docs before building.

16.8.1 Project 1: upgrade the lookalike job to be PU-aware

Goal: keep the existing lookalike ranking, but add honest audience sizing and a check on seed bias.

  1. Document the seed. Record where each seed member came from (CRM, loyalty program, campaign responders) as a column in the seed table in Unity Catalog.
  2. Compare seed with population. Before modeling, compare the seed’s distribution on key features (region, channel, device, age band) with the population’s. Large gaps warn that SCAR is doubtful. Report them to the client.
  3. Train the ranking model as usual (seed versus unlabeled, for example with gradient boosting), logged with MLflow.
  4. Add a sizing step. Estimate \(c\) on held-out seed members, as in the runnable example. Report the estimated audience size with a range. Flag the estimate as unreliable when the classes overlap heavily, which shows up as a low held-out AUC.
  5. Make the job reusable. Parameterize seed table, population table, and audience size; write scores and a run summary (size estimate, AUC, seed-gap report) to Delta tables.
  6. Validate with an experiment. For important clients, run a holdout or geo test comparing the new audience with the current method.

Deliverable: a ranked audience table plus a one-page run summary that a client lead can read.

16.8.2 Project 2: embedding-based lookalikes and recommendations

Goal: find similar users or products at scale, and explain why they are similar.

  1. Start simple. Before training a two-tower model, build user embeddings from existing features or from purchase sequences (for example, embeddings of product IDs averaged per user). This gives a baseline.
  2. Train a two-tower model when interaction data is large enough (clicks, purchases, views). Use in-batch negatives and a popularity correction.
  3. Index embeddings with Databricks AI Search (formerly Vector Search) for nearest-neighbor retrieval.
  4. Use multi-anchor search for lookalikes: cluster the seed embeddings and retrieve around each cluster center.
  5. Evaluate offline, then online. Offline: recall@K of held-out purchases, plus coverage and diversity of retrieved items. Online: an A/B test on conversion.
  6. Expose as a tool. Wrap “find lookalikes for seed X” as a Unity Catalog function or a job-triggering tool, so a Streamlit assistant can call it through MCP (Chapter 13).

Deliverable: an embedding table, a search index, and a retrieval tool with documented evaluation.

16.8.3 Project 3: high-volume text classification for voice of the customer

Goal: turn reviews, survey open-ends, or support emails into categorical features (topic, sentiment, urgency, refund request) cheaply and reliably.

  1. Define atomic questions. For example: topic (Choice), urgency (Score 0–4), refund request (yes or no). Write clear definitions and two or three examples for each label.
  2. Create a labeled sample. Have two people label 300 to 500 records and measure agreement. Low agreement means the label definitions need work before any model can do better.
  3. Compare candidate classifiers on the same sample: a generative LLM from Databricks Foundation Model APIs with structured output, a small fine-tuned encoder, and, if you have access, a typed decision model such as Jev. Compare accuracy, calibration, latency, and cost per 1,000 records.
  4. Calibrate and set thresholds with the cost rule above. Route the uncertain middle band to people.
  5. Run as a scheduled job that writes labels and probabilities to a Delta table, then feed those columns into dashboards or downstream models (churn, satisfaction drivers).
  6. Monitor monthly. Re-label a small random sample and re-check accuracy and calibration.

Deliverable: a labeled feature table, a model comparison report, and a monitoring check.

16.8.4 Project 4: a calibration check as a reusable tool

Goal: make “is this model’s probability trustworthy?” a standard step for every classifier the team ships.

  1. Package the calibration_report function from this chapter, plus AUC and the cost-based threshold, as a shared Python module.
  2. Add it to the team’s notebook template (Chapter 12) and to the evaluation step of every job, logging results to MLflow.
  3. Add a rule to the team’s AGENTS.md or skill: an agent may not recommend an automation threshold without a calibration report (Chapter 10).

Deliverable: one shared function and one team rule. This is the smallest project and a good first step.

16.8.5 Where to start

Start with Project 4, because it is small and improves every other project. Then do Project 1, which adds value to a job you already run. Projects 2 and 3 depend on data volume and access to labeled text, so scope them with a specific client use case.

16.9 Interview takeaways

  • “How would you build a lookalike model?” Frame it as PU learning. State the SCAR assumption, explain why ranking is unaffected by the missing negatives but sizing is not, and propose an experiment to validate lift.
  • “Why use a two-tower model?” It separates user and item encoders so that embeddings can be precomputed and searched with approximate nearest neighbors. The trade-off is weaker feature interactions, which a ranking stage recovers.
  • “Can I trust a model’s confidence score?” Only after measuring calibration on representative labeled data. Concentration of probability is not accuracy.
  • “LLM or classifier for ticket routing?” For stable categories at high volume, use a classifier or typed decision model with measured calibration. Reserve generative models for ambiguous cases and explanations.

16.10 Key takeaways

  • Lookalike modeling is a positive-unlabeled problem. The SCAR assumption determines what corrections are valid, and seed selection bias is often the bigger risk.
  • Two-tower networks make embedding-based retrieval and lookalikes scale; negative sampling and popularity bias need deliberate design.
  • Typed decision models such as Jev return structured probabilities instead of text. Their schema guarantees are credible by design, but accuracy and calibration claims require your own validation.
  • Confidence measures how concentrated a distribution is; calibration measures whether probabilities match reality. Automate only on measured calibration.
  • Validate in shadow mode first, then prove business value with experiments.
